PHIL 150 Philosophy of Technology, Fall 2026, 3 credits, Dr. Sarah Lindqvist. No prerequisites. A seminar on how technologies shape what we perceive, value and become, from ancient worries about writing to smartphones and surveillance.
Learning objectives: students will be able to (1) reconstruct a philosophical argument accurately and charitably, (2) distinguish instrumental from substantive views of technology, (3) apply a theory (Heidegger, Borgmann, Winner, de Beauvoir and others) to a technology from everyday life, and (4) write a thesis-driven essay that takes a counterargument seriously.

All readings are in the course reader PDF on the course site.
- Week 1 (Aug 25): what is philosophy of technology? Instrumental vs substantive views
- Week 2 (Sep 1): Plato, *Phaedrus* 274b–278b (the myth of Theuth, writing as pharmakon)
- Week 3 (Sep 8): Aristotle, *Physics* II.3 and *Nicomachean Ethics* VI.4 (techne, the four causes)
- Week 4 (Sep 15): Marx, "Fragment on Machines" (*Grundrisse*)
- Week 5 (Sep 22): Mumford, "Authoritarian and Democratic Technics"
- Week 6 (Sep 29): Heidegger, "The Question Concerning Technology", first half
- Week 7 (Oct 5): Heidegger, full text
- Week 8 (Oct 12): Borgmann, *Technology and the Character of Contemporary Life*, ch. 9 ("The Device Paradigm"); Essay 1 due Fri Oct 16

Second half of the syllabus (Essay 2 must use one of these besides de Beauvoir):
- Week 9 (Oct 19): Winner, "Do Artifacts Have Politics?"
- Week 10 (Oct 26): de Beauvoir, *The Ethics of Ambiguity*, part I
- Week 11 (Nov 2): Haraway, "A Cyborg Manifesto" (excerpt)
- Week 12 (Nov 9): Turkle, *Alone Together*, selected chapters
- Week 13 (Nov 16): Zuboff, *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism*, ch. 1; Essay 2 due Fri Nov 20
- Week 14 (Nov 24): Vallor, *Technology and the Virtues*, ch. 1 (Tue only, Thanksgiving break)
- Week 15 (Dec 1): synthesis and final reflection workshop
- Final reflection (1,000 words): due Wed Dec 9, 11:59 pm. No final exam.

Weights: Essay 1 25%, Essay 2 30%, reading responses 20%, participation 15%, final reflection 10%. Letter grades: A ≥ 93, A− ≥ 90, B+ ≥ 87, B ≥ 83, B− ≥ 80, C+ ≥ 77, C ≥ 73, C− ≥ 70, D ≥ 60, F < 60.
Essays are graded on four criteria, equally weighted: (1) a clear, arguable thesis, (2) accurate explanation of the author with direct citations, (3) quality of the application to a concrete technology, and (4) a serious counterargument and reply. Writing mechanics affect the grade only when they obscure the argument.

Seminar participation is 15% of the grade. Up to 3 absences need no explanation; each unexcused absence after that lowers the participation grade by one third of a letter. Participation means contributing to discussion, not just attending; a short written comment emailed after class counts for quieter students.
Late essays lose one third of a letter grade per day (A → A−). Extensions must be requested from Dr. Lindqvist at least 72 hours before the deadline. Reading responses are not accepted late, but the lowest two are dropped.

The ideas and prose in essays and responses must be your own. **AI-generated prose is not allowed** in any submitted work. AI tools may be used for brainstorming or to check your understanding of a reading, but only if disclosed in a footnote saying which tool and how it was used. Paraphrasing a source without citation is plagiarism, even when unintentional. Violations go to the Honor Council.

Citations: Chicago style footnotes. Cite the course reader editions with page numbers (Heidegger: the Lovitt translation). The Writing Center (Library 2nd floor) offers 45-minute appointments, and Dr. Lindqvist recommends one visit before Essay 1. Accommodation letters from the Disability Resource Center should be sent to Dr. Lindqvist (slindqvist@example.edu) early in term. Office hours: Thursdays 10:00–12:00, Humanities Building 118.
